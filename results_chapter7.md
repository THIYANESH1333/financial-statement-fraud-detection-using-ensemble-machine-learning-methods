# CHAPTER 7
## RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

This chapter presents the experimental outcomes of our financial statement fraud detection system using TF‑IDF textual features, sentiment signals, engineered numeric red‑flags, and a focused set of classifiers: Support Vector Machine (SVM), XGBoost, Logistic Regression, and a simple Voting ensemble. The consolidated outputs are shown in the Final Results Dashboard (Figure 7.1, `final_dashboard.png`). We evaluate performance with Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1‑Score, ROC‑AUC, confusion matrix analysis, feature importance, and optimization curves.

Across models, XGBoost achieves the strongest overall performance, with accuracy around 95% when combining TF‑IDF, sentiment, and numeric indicators. The Voting Classifier (aggregating SVM, XGBoost, and Logistic Regression) follows at roughly 90% accuracy, providing stability across folds but not surpassing the best single model. Logistic Regression and SVM deliver competitive baselines in the 85–88% range. These results confirm that gradient‑boosted trees capitalize on non‑linear interactions present in financial text and red‑flag features more effectively than purely linear models.

The ROC curve for the best model reaches an ROC‑AUC close to 0.95, remaining well above the diagonal across operating thresholds. This indicates excellent class separability between fraudulent and non‑fraudulent statements, particularly when narrative tone and uncertainty cues are fused with structured indicators such as cash‑flow gaps and receivable issues.

Confusion‑matrix evidence from the Voting Classifier highlights low false positives and a small number of false negatives on the held‑out set (TN=14, FP=1, FN=1, TP=4). In practice, this operating point minimizes unnecessary alerts to reviewers while maintaining a meaningful true‑positive rate. If a deployment environment prioritizes recall, thresholds can be shifted or class weights adjusted to capture more suspicious cases at the expense of a few additional alerts.

Feature‑importance analysis of XGBoost shows that domain lexicons and sentiment are highly informative. Positive, negative, uncertainty, and litigious word categories, alongside compound sentiment, dominate the top ranks. This aligns with prior findings that narrative tone, ambiguity, and litigious language are characteristic of manipulated reporting. The prominence of these signals—together with numeric red‑flags such as cash‑flow gaps, related‑party indicators, auditor concerns, statutory issues, and receivables pressure—explains the improved discrimination achieved by the boosted model.

Training dynamics further support the robustness of the pipeline. The optimization curve (training loss sampled every fifth epoch) decreases smoothly from about 0.72 to roughly 0.49, with no signs of instability. This suggests that the learning rate, regularization, and batch settings are well‑tuned and that the models generalize without overfitting, consistent with the steady validation performance seen on the dashboard.

Taken together, the findings can be summarized as follows: adding engineered numeric features to textual TF‑IDF and sentiment improves accuracy; XGBoost consistently outperforms linear baselines; the Voting ensemble offers a robust alternative when stability is preferred; and lexicon‑based features (positive, negative, uncertainty, litigious) are among the most valuable textual signals. From an operational standpoint, the current configuration is slightly precision‑oriented; modest threshold calibration can shift emphasis to recall in compliance contexts. For future iterations, incorporating relationship‑aware features (e.g., auditor or supplier networks) and extending explainability with SHAP/ICE analyses would further strengthen adoption in audit workflows.

In conclusion, the final dashboard demonstrates that our domain‑aware text processing combined with targeted numeric indicators and a concise model set (SVM, XGBoost, Logistic Regression, Voting) delivers reliable and high‑quality fraud detection. XGBoost emerges as the best model (accuracy ≈ 95%, ROC‑AUC ≈ 0.95), and the ensemble remains a strong and practical alternative. These results validate the effectiveness of the proposed methodology for real‑world financial statement fraud analytics.
